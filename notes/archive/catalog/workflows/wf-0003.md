# Risk and tech-debt inventory

## Goal

A ranked list where the ranking survives an argument. Every item carries a **consequence**, a
**trigger** (what has to happen for the consequence to occur), and a **cost to fix**. The top
five become the raised list in `wf-0002`; the rest becomes the appendix.

The real output is a **separation**: what is genuinely risky from what is merely unpleasant to
work in. Those get conflated constantly, and conflating them is how a consultant ends up
recommending a rewrite.

## Preconditions

- `wf-0001` complete. You cannot rank risk in a system you cannot narrate.
- An **inbox** of raw observations collected *during* orientation — every "huh" moment, unsorted,
  unjudged. Start the file on day one; the observations that matter are the ones you notice
  before you get used to the system. After four days you stop seeing them.

## The severity model

Rank on **expected business cost**, not on technical severity. Three factors, deliberately
coarse — precision here is false precision:

| Factor | Question | Scale |
|---|---|---|
| **Consequence** | If this fires, what does the business lose? | Revenue stops · Data lost · Customer trust · Team velocity · Nothing measurable |
| **Trigger** | What has to happen for it to fire, and how likely is that in 12 months? | Already happening · Routine event (a deploy, a traffic spike, a person leaving) · Unusual event · Needs a coincidence |
| **Blast radius** | How much is affected, and can you recover? | Unrecoverable · All customers · Some customers · One team |

**Ranking rule:** anything whose trigger is *already happening* or *a routine event* outranks
everything whose trigger needs an unusual event, regardless of consequence. Consultants
systematically over-rank exotic catastrophes and under-rank the boring thing that fires every
third deploy — and the boring one is what the client is already paying for, invisibly.

**The "nothing measurable" row is not a filler.** Items that land there are not findings. They
are preferences, and they go in a separate list that never leaves your machine.

## Steps

### 1. Empty the inbox, unjudged *(20 min)*

Every raw observation from orientation into rows. Do not filter yet. Filtering while collecting
is how the interesting-but-hard-to-articulate items get lost.

### 2. Add the mechanical sweeps *(45 min, agent-assisted)*

The classes an agent finds reliably because they are structural (`dec-0003` — trust, spot-check):

- Dependencies with known advisories, and how far behind the majors are.
- Secrets, credentials or customer data in git history — **not just the working tree.**
- Auth and authorisation checks: which entry points from `wf-0001` step 4 have none.
- Error handling on external calls: unretried, untimed-out, unbounded.
- Data integrity: missing constraints, nullable columns holding meaning, missing uniqueness.
- Backup and restore: whether a restore has ever been tested. Usually it has not.
- Single points of human failure, from the `git log` author analysis.

**Done when:** each finding has a `file:line` or a command output behind it. No finding survives
into the inventory on the agent's word alone.

### 3. Ask the humans the two questions the code cannot answer *(30 min)*

- *"What breaks most often?"* — gives you the *already happening* triggers, which top the
  ranking and which no static analysis will find.
- *"What are you afraid to touch?"* — gives you the blast-radius items, and it is the single
  most productive question in this workflow.

### 4. Verify every candidate top-five item personally *(60 min)*

The five that will be raised (`dec-0004`) get read by hand. Assume the client's most defensive
engineer will contest each one. Anything you cannot defend from something you read or ran
yourself drops out of the top five — it does not get softened, it drops.

### 5. Rank, then sanity-check the ranking *(30 min)*

Apply the model. Then check the result against one question: **"If I could only fix one, would
the business notice?"** If the answer for your number one is "not really", the ranking is wrong
— you have almost certainly ranked something by how much it offends you.

### 6. Split into raised list and appendix *(15 min)*

Top five out to `wf-0002` with consequence and cost sentences. Everything else to the appendix,
marked unranked. Security exposures and data-loss risks skip the whole document and go out the
same day (`dec-0004`).

## Failure modes

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Top of the list is a rewrite recommendation | Ranked by unpleasantness, not consequence | Apply the trigger rule. "It is hard to work in" has a velocity consequence at most, and rarely a trigger. |
| Forty items, all "high" | No consequence discipline | An item with no nameable business consequence is a preference. Separate list, never delivered. |
| Client's engineer refutes item #2 in the meeting | Step 4 skipped for that item | Personally verify all five. Every time. |
| Inventory misses the thing that breaks in month three | Only did the mechanical sweeps | Step 3. Humans know the *already happening* triggers; the code does not record them. |
| Security finding buried at #4 in a document delivered next Tuesday | Treated as an inventory item | Security and data loss are out-of-band, same day. Not capped, not queued. |
| Every item is about the code, none about the org | Only read the repository | Deploy process, on-call, bus factor and restore-testing are risks. They are usually the top-ranked ones. |
