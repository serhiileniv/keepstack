# The week-one written assessment

## Goal

A document the founder reads to the end, forwards to one other person, and funds something out
of. It should leave them with **one thing they did not already know**, a short list they can
pay for, and a clear sense that the rate was correct.

It is not a report on the codebase. It is a decision aid for someone who cannot read the
codebase and has to spend money anyway.

## Preconditions

- `wf-0001` complete: map, money path traced, unknowns answered or marked.
- `wf-0003` complete: the full inventory exists, severity-ranked.
- `dec-0004` applied: you know which five items you are raising, and each has a business
  consequence and a rough cost.
- You know **who else will read it.** A document for the founder alone and a document that goes
  to a board or an investor are different documents. Ask before writing.

## Structure

Six sections, in this order, and the order is doing work. Two to four pages. If it is longer
than four, the raised list has more than five items and you have not applied `dec-0004`.

1. **What I did, and what I did not look at.** Four sentences. Naming the boundary up front is
   what makes everything after it trustworthy, and it retires the "did you check X?" question
   before it is asked.
2. **What is working.** Genuinely, specifically, with names. Two or three items. Not a courtesy
   paragraph — it is the section that proves you read the actual system rather than pattern-
   matching a type of system, and it buys you the standing to write section 4.
3. **How it works, in one page.** The map from `wf-0001`, in the founder's vocabulary. One
   diagram if it helps. This section exists so that section 4 lands somewhere.
4. **The five things.** Each: what it is, **what it costs the business and when**, roughly what
   fixing it takes, and what happens if you do nothing. Ordered by consequence, not by severity
   score, and not by cost.
5. **What I would do in the first 90 days.** Sequenced, with the first two weeks concrete. This
   is the section that converts an assessment into a retainer, and the one most often missing.
6. **Appendix: the full inventory.** Handed over, explicitly marked *unranked, not a to-do list*
   (`dec-0004`).

## Steps

### 1. Write section 4 first *(60 min)*

Start with the five raised items, before any framing. If you cannot state each one's business
consequence in a single sentence without hedging, it does not belong on the list — go back to
`wf-0003` and take the next item down.

**Done when:** five items, five consequence sentences, no item whose only justification is
technical preference.

### 2. Find the one non-obvious finding and put it first *(30 min)*

Of the five, exactly one should be something the founder's own team would not have told them.
It usually came out of the money-path trace or from question 3 in `wf-0001` step 8.

If you do not have one, **say so** in section 1 rather than dressing up a known problem as a
discovery. Founders can tell, and the attempt costs more than the absence.

**Done when:** you can name why the internal team had not raised it. That reason is often the
more important finding — nobody owns it, nobody is measured on it, or the person who would raise
it does not have the founder's ear.

### 3. Draft sections 1–3 and 5 *(60 min, agent-assisted)*

Use `skill-0002` to draft from `map.md` and the inventory. Constrain it explicitly:

- No adjectives about code quality. Consequences only.
- No recommendations in sections 1–3.
- Vocabulary check against the niche brief: engagement, client, risk, cost, consequence. Never
  "best practice", never "leverage", never "modernise".

**Done when:** a non-technical reader can restate section 3 back to you.

### 4. Run the verification pass *(45 min — the step that protects the rate)*

Per `dec-0003`: go line by line and mark every claim that is a **number**, an **absence**, or a
**production behaviour**. Verify each one personally, or remove it.

Then do a second pass for the word "no" — "there is no...", "nothing does...", "they don't have
any..." Absence claims are the ones that get disputed in the room.

**Done when:** every remaining number came from a command you ran, and every absence claim was
confirmed by a human or by an exhaustive search you can reproduce.

### 5. Cut it by a third *(30 min)*

Everything that is true, correct and does not change what the founder does next comes out. Most
of what you cut will be the explanations you were proudest of.

**Done when:** every remaining paragraph would be missed if it were deleted.

### 6. Deliver it in a meeting, not by email *(45 min)*

Send it 24 hours ahead. Walk section 4, take questions, and end by asking which of the five they
want to start. Then send a one-paragraph follow-up naming the decision they made.

**Done when:** the follow-up email exists. The written record of the decision is what makes the
document a plan rather than a report.

## Failure modes

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Founder reads it, says "very thorough", funds nothing | No consequence sentences; findings stated as technical facts | Section 4, every item, money and time. "Deploys are manual" is not a finding; "any deploy needs the one person who knows the runbook, and he is on leave in March" is. |
| Founder gets defensive | Findings read as criticism of a named person | `dec-0004` — raise the class, not the instance. Also strengthen section 2. |
| Internal engineer disputes a claim | Verification pass skipped or partial | Step 4. Every number from a command, every absence confirmed. |
| Document is nine pages | Raised list is not capped | Five items. The appendix is where the rest lives. |
| It reads like it was generated | Adjectives, symmetry, and no specific file, name or number in it | Specificity is the tell in both directions. Name the file, the person, the count. |
| No renewal despite a good document | Section 5 missing or vague | The 90-day plan is the commercial half of the deliverable. |
