# Which tech debt to raise, and which to absorb

## Context

The inventory from `wf-0003` has forty-odd items. The assessment is due tomorrow. Every item on
that list is real.

Two things are true at once. Reporting all of it makes you look thorough and makes the founder
feel ambushed — they cannot fund forty things, so they fund none and quietly conclude the system
is a disaster and possibly that hiring you was the moment they found out. And roughly half of
those items are, in practice, criticism of a named person who still works there, sometimes the
person who introduced you.

You are not deciding what is true. You are deciding **what to say**, which is a different
decision and the one nobody writes down.

## Options

| Option | Real trade-off |
|---|---|
| **A. Report everything — full transparency** | Defensible, thorough, and unusable. Also a political device you did not intend to deploy: a forty-item list with the original author's name implicitly on half of it is an act, whatever your intent. |
| **B. Report only what threatens revenue in the next 90 days** | Immediately actionable and gets funded. You own the omissions: when something you saw and didn't mention breaks in month five, "it was in my notes" is not a defence. |
| **C. Two tiers — a short raised list, plus the full inventory handed over in writing but not presented** | Actionable *and* covered. Costs an extra hour of writing and requires the founder to accept a document you have told them not to read yet. |
| **D. Raise nothing in week one; earn trust first** | Avoids all political risk. Also skips the deliverable you were hired for, and the honeymoon is exactly when a founder is most willing to fund unpleasant work. |

## The call

**C, with a hard cap of five raised items.**

- **Raise at most five.** Each one gets a **business consequence** and a **rough cost**. No
  consequence you can name in a sentence → it does not go on the raised list.
- **Hand over the full inventory as an appendix**, and say in writing that it is unranked, not
  prioritised for action, and not a to-do list. That sentence is what makes the appendix safe to
  give them.
- **Two carve-outs override the cap.** An active security exposure or a live data-loss risk goes
  up immediately, out of band, the day you find it — not in the document, not on the list, not
  capped. And anything that blocks the engagement itself gets raised regardless.
- **If the author is still employed and in the room, raise the class, not the instance.** "Error
  handling is inconsistent across the payment services" — not "the retry logic in
  `PaymentsController` is wrong." Same finding, and only one of them makes you an enemy on day
  four.

## Why

**Five is the number a non-technical founder can hold and fund.** Not a metaphor — the raised
list becomes a slide, a board line item, or a budget conversation, and those have room for about
five things. Beyond that the list stops being a plan and becomes a mood.

**Every item without a named consequence devalues the items that have one.** The finding whose
only justification is "this is not how I would have done it" costs you nothing to write and costs
your most important finding some of its weight, because the reader is now averaging across your
list. It also marks you, quickly and permanently, as the consultant who criticises — which is
the cheapest reputation to acquire in this job and the hardest to lose.

What was tried and failed:

- **The eighteen-item ranked list.** The founder read it, felt responsible, and funded the three
  cheapest items — none of which mattered. Ranking did not save it; length defeated the ranking.
  A list long enough to require a ranking is long enough to be re-ranked by the reader on cost.
- **Naming the instance with the author present.** Technically correct, precisely sourced, and it
  bought a defensive engineering lead for the remaining four months. Everything after that
  arrived pre-argued. The class-level version of the same finding got fixed.
- **Saying nothing in week one to build trust first** (option D). By month two the founder had
  adjusted to the pace and the same findings landed as "why didn't you say this earlier?" The
  honeymoon is a spending window and it closes.
- **Absorbing an item silently and fixing it yourself.** Sometimes correct, and it has a trap:
  you have now made yourself the reason the thing works. Silent absorption is for things that
  cost you minutes. Anything that costs days gets raised, even if you were going to do it anyway
  — especially then, because that is billable work you just gave away.

**What "absorb silently" actually means:** it is not "hide it". It is *goes in the appendix,
not in the conversation.* The written record is complete; the spoken agenda is five items long.
That distinction is what makes this a defensible position rather than a convenient one.

## When this is wrong

- **Technical due diligence for an acquirer.** Report everything, ranked, with no cap. The
  buyer's downside *is* the deliverable and omission is malpractice. Opposite decision entirely.
- **Security and data-loss findings.** Never capped, never softened, never held for the document.
  Same day, in writing, to whoever can act.
- **When you are the interim CTO rather than an advisor.** You now own the backlog. The full
  inventory is your plan, not an appendix, and the audience for it is the team, not the founder.
- **When the founder is technical and asks for the whole list.** Give them the whole list. They
  can hold it, and refusing reads as managing them.
- **Regulated environments with a duty to report.** Compliance obligations outrank
  communication strategy, always.

## Expiry trigger

This is a judgement decision, not a tooling one — no model release invalidates it. It expires on
**changes to the engagement shape**, and those are the things to watch:

- You take on M&A or due-diligence work, where the reporting duty is inverted.
- You move from advisory to interim, where you own the backlog you are reporting.
- Your assessment format changes such that the "raised list" is no longer a spoken conversation.
- A client pushes back on the cap — *"is this everything?"* — often enough that five is the wrong
  number for the kind of client you now have. Record the pushback; it is the signal.
