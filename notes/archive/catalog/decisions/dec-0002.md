# How deep to go in week one

## Context

You have been retained. Week one is orientation: an unfamiliar codebase, an unfamiliar org, and
a founder who is watching to see whether the rate was a good idea.

The ramp is the most expensive week of the engagement and usually the least billable — absorbed,
discounted, or hidden inside a fixed monthly fee. Every extra day you spend reading is a day of
effective rate you never get back. But the week-one assessment is also the single artifact that
decides whether month two happens.

So: more depth costs you money now and buys you renewal later, and there is a point where it
stops buying anything at all. Where is it?

## Options

| Option | Real trade-off |
|---|---|
| **A. Breadth-first, ~2 days, whole system shallow** | Cheapest ramp, and you can speak to any part of the system in a meeting. The findings sound like findings anyone could have produced — because they are. A founder who has hired a consultant before has heard all of them. |
| **B. Breadth-first map, then one deep vertical slice** | Costs about a day more than A. The deep slice is the only part of the deliverable that proves you actually read *their* code. Risk: you pick the wrong slice and spend the day somewhere that doesn't matter. |
| **C. Full audit, 8–10 days** | Genuinely comprehensive, and correct when the audit *is* the deliverable. Otherwise you spend the goodwill of week one invisible, reading, and hand over a document longer than the client's capacity to act on it. |
| **D. Depth-first from day one on whatever they complained about in the sales call** | Fastest path to a visible win, and sometimes exactly right. Usually you discover in week three that what they complained about was a symptom, and you have no map to locate the cause. |

## The call

**B, hard time-boxed: breadth in one day, one deep vertical slice in two, then stop.**

Cap the whole ramp at **three days, or ~20% of month one**, whichever is smaller. Pick the
vertical slice by one criterion only: **the path where a failure costs the business money**
— checkout, signup, the ingestion pipeline, whatever their revenue actually flows through.
Not the ugliest code, not the part they complain about most.

When the three days are up, you stop reading and start writing, whether or not you feel ready.
Feeling ready is not a signal; it arrives around day eight regardless of the system.

## Why

Renewal is not decided by coverage. It is decided by whether the founder hears **one thing they
did not already know and could not have got from their own team.** One is the number. Two is a
bonus. Coverage produces zero of them, reliably, because everything a shallow pass finds is
something the team has already told them.

That one thing comes almost exclusively from the deep slice — from having followed a real
request through the actual code to the actual database, and noticed the thing nobody mentions
because everyone stopped seeing it years ago.

What was tried and failed:

- **The full audit.** Ten days, forty findings, a document nobody finished reading. The client
  actioned three items, all of them cheap, none of them the important ones. Worse: for two weeks
  the founder's only experience of the engagement was silence. Week one is when you are being
  evaluated on presence, not just output.
- **Pure breadth with an agent.** Fast, wide, and it reads like what it is. An agent-generated
  survey has a texture that founders who have seen a few of them now recognise, and once they
  recognise it they discount the whole document — including the parts you actually verified.
  See `dec-0003`.
- **Picking the deep slice by ugliness.** The worst code is usually old, stable and untouched,
  which is *why* it is ugly. Nobody is going to pay to fix it and nothing bad is happening.
  Revenue path, every time.
- **Extending the ramp "just two more days".** It is never two days, and the extension is always
  spent on the second-most-important thing. The time-box is doing real work by being arbitrary.

The economics, stated plainly: at a typical fractional rate, each unbillable ramp day costs a
four-figure sum. Days one to three buy the finding. Days four to ten buy confidence — yours, not
theirs — and confidence is not a deliverable.

## When this is wrong

- **Technical due diligence.** The audit *is* the product and it is billed. Go to C and go
  wide; the buyer's downside is the deliverable. Different engagement, different decision.
- **A client in an active incident or an active outage pattern.** Go depth-first on the incident
  path immediately (option D). The map can wait; the bleeding cannot, and fixing it in week one
  is worth more than any assessment.
- **Engagements shorter than a month.** There is no renewal to earn. Skip the assessment
  entirely and deliver one concrete thing they can keep.
- **Interim or hands-on-keyboard roles**, as opposed to advisory. If you are going to be writing
  code in this system for six months, the deep pass amortises and the time-box is
  counterproductive.
- **When the founder is technical.** They will have already given you the one non-obvious thing
  in the first conversation. Skip ahead — your value is in prioritisation, not discovery.

## Expiry trigger

- **A step-change in the cost of whole-repo depth.** This decision exists because breadth and
  depth trade off against each other. If a full deep pass over a mid-sized repo becomes an
  hour of agent time rather than days of reading — cheaper long-context, reliable repo-wide
  retrieval, agents that execute the test suite to confirm what they claim — then the trade-off
  disappears and the correct answer moves toward C. **Re-test this every time context pricing
  drops by an order of magnitude.**
- Your own engagement shape changing: advisory to interim, or monthly retainer to fixed-fee
  project, changes who pays for the ramp and therefore changes the answer.
